Bhagavad Gita: The Divine Song of Wisdom and Action

Chapter 2: The Yoga of Knowledge (Sankhya Yoga)
The soul is never born, nor does it ever die. It has not come into being, does not come into being, and will not come into being. It is unborn, eternal, everlasting, and primeval. It is not slain when the body is slain. Just as a person sheds worn-out garments and wears new ones, so the embodied soul casts off worn-out bodies and enters new ones.

Weapons cannot cleave the soul, fire cannot burn it, water cannot wet it, and wind cannot dry it. It is eternal, all-pervading, unchangeable, immovable, and primordial. Knowing the Self to be such, you have no cause to grieve for the physical vessel.

Do your prescribed duty with steadfastness. You have a right only to work, never to its fruits. Let not the fruits of action be your motive, nor let your attachment be to inaction. Established in Yoga, perform your duties, casting off attachment and remaining even-minded in success and failure. This equanimity of mind is called Yoga.

When a person completely casts away all desires of the mind and finds satisfaction in the true Self alone, then is that person said to be of steady wisdom (Sthitaprajna). One whose mind is undisturbed by sorrow, who has no craving for pleasures, and who is free from passion, fear, and anger, is called a sage of settled understanding.

Chapter 3: The Yoga of Action (Karma Yoga)
No one can remain even for a moment without performing action. The gunas of material nature compel all living beings to act. One who restrains the senses of action but continues to dwell on sense objects in the mind is self-deluded. But one who controls the senses with the mind and engages the organs of action in selfless work without attachment is far superior.

Perform your prescribed duty, for action is indeed better than inaction. Even the maintenance of your body cannot be accomplished without action. Work done as a selfless offering does not bind the soul. Therefore, perform your duty efficiently and selflessly for the welfare of all beings (Lokasangraha).

Whatever a great person does, others follow. Whatever standards they set by example, the world adopts. Therefore, the wise should work without attachment, seeking only the upliftment and order of the world. Better is one’s own duty, though devoid of merit, than the duty of another well performed. Doing one’s own natural duty brings peace.

Chapter 4: The Path of Wisdom and Sacrificial Action (Jnana Karma Sanyasa Yoga)
Whenever righteousness declines and unrighteousness prevails, the divine consciousness manifests to protect the good, transform ignorance, and re-establish the balance of truth (Dharma).

He who sees inaction in action, and action in inaction, is wise among humans. He is a yogi who has accomplished all action. One whose undertakings are devoid of selfish desire and motive, whose actions are purified in the fire of spiritual wisdom, is called a sage by the learned.

There is no purifier in this world like divine knowledge. One who is perfected in yoga finds this wisdom within the Self in due course of time. The person endowed with faith, seeking wisdom, and possessing mastery over the senses attains supreme knowledge and swiftly enters into profound peace.

Chapter 6: The Yoga of Meditation (Dhyana Yoga)
Let a person lift oneself by one's own Self; let not one degrade oneself. For the Self alone is the friend of oneself, and the Self alone is one's own enemy. For one who has conquered the lower self by the higher Self, the Self is a friend; but for one who has not conquered the mind, the self remains an adversary like an external enemy.

When the disciplined mind rests solely in the Self, freed from craving for all desires, then one is said to be established in Yoga. As a lamp placed in a windless place does not flicker, even so is the subdued mind of the yogi absorbed in meditation on the Self.

The mind is restless, turbulent, obstinate, and strong. Mastering it seems as difficult as holding back the wind. Yet, by constant practice (abhyasa) and detachment (vairagya), it can be mastered. One who sees the Divine in all things and all things in the Divine is never separated from peace, nor is peace ever separated from them.

Chapter 12: The Yoga of Devotion (Bhakti Yoga)
He who has no ill will toward any being, who is friendly and compassionate to all, free from possessiveness and egotism, equal in pleasure and pain, forgiving, ever content, steady in meditation, self-controlled, and firm in conviction — such a devotee is deeply dear to the Divine.

He who does not agitate the world and who is not agitated by the world, who is freed from excitement, impatience, fear, and anxiety, is peaceful and wise. He who is indifferent to blame and praise, silent, content with anything that comes, unattached to home, and steady of mind — such a person is grounded in truth.

Chapter 18: The Yoga of Liberation and Renunciation (Moksha Sanyasa Yoga)
Renunciation of selfish acts is termed sannyasa by sages; the surrender of the fruits of all work is declared to be tyaga by the wise. Acts of sacrifice, charity, and self-discipline should never be abandoned; they must be performed, for they purify the wise.

Knowledge that sees the one undivided, imperishable reality in all separated beings is sattvic knowledge. Action that is orderly, performed without attachment, without love or hatred, and without longing for rewards is pure action. Steadiness by which one controls the activities of the mind, life energy, and senses through unswerving yoga is sattvic determination.

Surrender all your actions to the supreme truth, make that truth your highest goal, and take refuge in the yoga of discernment. With your mind thus focused, you will overcome all obstacles by grace. Be fearless, cultivate purity of heart, remain steadfast in wisdom, practice charity, mastery over senses, gentle speech, and harmony with all life.
